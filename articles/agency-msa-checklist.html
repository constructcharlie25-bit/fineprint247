What to Check Before Signing a Client MSA: An Agency & Consultant Checklist

Published October 7, 2026 · 7 min read

A new client sends their “standard” Master Services Agreement. The project is exciting, the budget is real, and the client wants to start Monday. You are one signature away from revenue.

Here's the thing agencies learn the expensive way: a Statement of Work governs one project, but the MSA governs every project you'll ever do for that client. It sets the standing rules for payment, IP, liability, and termination for years. A bad SOW costs you a quarter. A bad MSA costs you a client relationship's worth of margin.

Below: the nine clauses worth checking before you sign, in a simple pattern — what it says, what it costs you, what to ask for.

1. Scope & change control: where scope creep becomes legal

What it says

“Agency shall perform all Services reasonably necessary or incidental to the completion of the project, as determined by Client.”

What it costs you

This turns scope creep into a contractual obligation. If the client decides the project “reasonably” includes three extra deliverables, you're on the hook — and there's no mechanism to charge for them.

What to ask for

Tie everything to a written SOW and require change orders: “Services are limited to those described in an executed Statement of Work. Additional work requires a written change order, signed by both parties, specifying price and timeline impact.”

2. IP ownership: protect your toolkit

What it says

“All Work Product, including any pre-existing intellectual property incorporated therein, shall be deemed ‘work made for hire’ and is hereby assigned to Client.”

What it costs you

For an agency, this is the most expensive sentence in the MSA. Your reusable components, internal tools, frameworks, and templates — the assets you deploy across every client — get handed to one client, forever. The next client you use them for becomes an infringement risk.

What to ask for

Carve out your pre-existing and reusable materials, and grant the client a license to the rest: “Agency retains all right, title, and interest in its pre-existing materials, tools, and reusable components. Client receives a perpetual, non-exclusive license to use them as incorporated into the deliverables.”

3. Payment terms: the agency cash-flow killer

What it says

“Client shall pay undisputed invoices within sixty (60) days of receipt. No interest shall accrue on late payments.”

What it costs you

Net-60 with no late penalty means you are financing the client's operations with your payroll. For an agency running on monthly burn, two slow-paying clients can create a genuine cash crisis — and the MSA gives you no leverage to speed them up.

What to ask for

Push for Net 30, add 1.5% monthly interest on overdue invoices, and negotiate an upfront deposit (25–50%) on new engagements. If the client balks at interest, at minimum get a right to pause work on invoices more than 30 days overdue.

4. Termination: who can walk away, and what gets paid

What it says

“Client may terminate this Agreement or any SOW for convenience upon thirty (30) days’ written notice.”

What it costs you

One-sided termination for convenience means the client can drop you mid-project and you're left with staffed capacity and no revenue. Worse, without a payment-on-termination clause, you may not get paid for work in progress.

What to ask for

Make termination mutual, require payment for all work performed through the termination date plus a kill fee (e.g., 25–50% of the remaining SOW value), and cap “transition assistance” at a defined number of hours — open-ended transition obligations are free consulting.

5. Liability caps & indemnification: the uncapped disaster

What it says

“Agency shall indemnify and hold harmless Client from any and all claims arising from the Services, without limitation as to amount. Agency's total liability shall not exceed the fees paid under this Agreement.”

What it costs you

Read that pair carefully: your liability is capped, but your indemnification obligation is uncapped — and indemnification is where the real money lives. One third-party claim (a stock photo dispute, an accessibility lawsuit) can exceed everything you've ever billed the client.

What to ask for

Cap the indemnity too — ideally at the same level as the liability cap (fees paid in the 12 months preceding the claim). Narrow indemnification to third-party IP claims arising from your work, and make it mutual: the client indemnifies you for claims arising from content and materials they supplied.

6. Non-solicitation & non-compete: handcuffs on your team

What it says

“During the term and for twenty-four (24) months thereafter, Agency shall not solicit or hire any employee of Client, nor provide services to any competitor of Client.”

What it costs you

A 24-month non-compete against “any competitor of Client” can quietly disqualify you from your own market — for an agency, your client's competitors are your prospects. And a one-sided non-solicit means the client can poach your staff while you can't hire theirs.

What to ask for

Make non-solicitation mutual, shorten it to 12 months, and narrow any non-compete to named competitors in an exhibit — or delete it outright. A services business that can't serve its market isn't a business.

7. Warranties: don't promise what you can't control

What it says

“Agency warrants that all deliverables shall be free from defects and fit for their intended purpose.”

What it costs you

“Fit for intended purpose” is an unlimited warranty — it makes you responsible for outcomes you don't control, including how the client uses what you built. A bug in production six months later becomes your warranty claim.

What to ask for

Limit warranties to professional performance (“performed in a professional and workmanlike manner”) with a defined warranty period (30–90 days from delivery), and expressly disclaim responsibility for client-supplied content, third-party tools, and the client's own use of the deliverables.

8. Insurance: the hidden line item

What it says

“Agency shall maintain commercial general liability insurance of not less than $2,000,000 per occurrence, professional liability insurance of not less than $2,000,000, and cyber liability insurance of not less than $5,000,000.”

What it costs you

Enterprise-grade insurance minimums are a real annual cost — five figures for a small shop — and they're often copy-pasted from the client's vendor template without any connection to your project. Sign blindly and you've added a permanent overhead line.

What to ask for

Negotiate the limits down to what your project actually warrants, or ask the client to cover the premium delta. At minimum, price the insurance cost into your proposal before you sign.

9. The 15-minute pre-sign routine

You don't need to redline the whole MSA yourself — you need to find the three clauses that matter most and decide your asks. Here's the routine:

  1. Search the payment terms first. Find “Net”, “days of receipt”, and “interest”. Net-60+ with no late penalty is your first negotiation target.
  2. Search “pre-existing” and “work made for hire”. If your toolkit isn't carved out, nothing else in the MSA matters as much as fixing this.
  3. Search “indemnif” and “without limitation”. Uncapped indemnification is the clause that can exceed your total revenue from the client.
  4. Search “terminate”. Confirm termination is mutual, work-in-progress gets paid, and transition assistance is capped.
  5. Search “non-compete” and “non-solicit”. Confirm they're mutual, short (12 months), and don't lock you out of your own market.

When to get a lawyer involved

Rule of thumb: if the MSA will govern more than roughly $25,000 of annual client work, an hour of a contract lawyer's time pays for itself. Bring your marked-up draft and your three asks — you're paying for a 30-minute review of your redlines, not a from-scratch rewrite. And for the bet-the-agency client, the one whose revenue would hurt to lose, get the lawyer before you sign, not after the dispute starts.
